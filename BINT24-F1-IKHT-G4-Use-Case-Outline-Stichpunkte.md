**Professional Backgrounds, Expertise and Responsibilities:**

All six developers work **full-stack** — there is no fixed split into frontend, backend, QA or DevOps. Every developer can pick up any item from the sprint backlog.

| Role | Function / Responsibility | Expertise level | Derived from |
| ---- | ------------------------- | --------------- | ------------ |
| PO | Backlog ownership, stakeholder management, prioritisation, acceptance of increments | Product side, not implementing | Coordinator, Completer Finisher |
| SM | Process facilitation, removing impediments, protecting the team, moderating the events | Agile coaching, not implementing | Teamworker, Coordinator, Resource Investigator |
| Dev1 | Full-stack development + deep expert knowledge (mobile performance, sensor/health-data integration) | **Senior specialist** | Belbin *Specialist* |
| Dev2 | Full-stack development, drives topics forward | Experienced generalist | Shaper, Implementer, Teamworker |
| Dev3 | Full-stack development + deep expert knowledge (backend architecture, data protection) | **Senior specialist** | Belbin *Specialist* |
| Dev4 | Full-stack development, executing tasks | Junior generalist | Implementer, Teamworker |
| Dev5 | Full-stack development, executing tasks | Generalist | Implementer, Teamworker |
| Dev6 | Full-stack development, external contacts, tool/library scouting | Generalist | Resource Investigator, Shaper |

- Seniority is **not** defined by the org chart but derived from the Belbin roles: only Dev1 and Dev3 carry the *Specialist* role and are therefore the two recognised technical experts of the team.
- Consequence 1 (strength): full-stack staffing removes single points of failure and allows work to be handed over across time zones ("follow the sun").
- Consequence 2 (risk): no fixed ownership means **nobody is formally accountable for a component**, which reinforces the role confusion and the unclear decision-making already identified below.
- Consequence 3 (risk): there is **no dedicated QA role**. Quality relies entirely on the developers themselves — which combines badly with the missing *Monitor Evaluator* (no critical review) and the overrepresentation of *Completer Finisher* (detail work without an independent quality gate).
- Consequence 4 (conflict potential): the two informal experts (Dev1, 55 and Dev3, 65, both India, PDI 77) are the oldest members of the team and report to a 30-year-old female PO and a 45-year-old female SM from low power distance cultures. **Expertise, age and gender authority therefore point in opposite directions** — this intensifies the authority tension described in the conflict section.

**Educational Diversity:**

All members have a technical education, but they were socialised in four different national education systems:

| Country | Typical educational path | Learned expectations towards work |
| ------- | ------------------------ | --------------------------------- |
| Germany (PO, Dev5) | University / university of applied sciences, dual study system, strong theory combined with structured practical phases | Documentation, planning, "principles first", process orientation |
| USA (SM, Dev6) | Bachelor's degree with broad general education plus CS major, project- and portfolio-driven | Presentation skills, self-marketing, pragmatic "applications first" approach |
| India (Dev1, Dev3) | B.Tech / M.Tech at engineering colleges, highly competitive entrance examinations, strong mathematical and theoretical foundation | Teacher- and authority-centred learning, exam orientation, respect for hierarchy and seniority |
| Japan (Dev2, Dev4) | University degree followed by intensive on-the-job training in the company, long onboarding phases | Group harmony, seniority-based progression, learning by observation rather than by asking |

- Educational diversity is a **second, independent source of the communication problems**: teacher-centred systems (IN/JP) train people not to question the authority in the room, while discussion-based systems (DE/US) reward exactly that behaviour.
- This explains why silence in a meeting is read as agreement by the German and US members, while for the Indian and Japanese members it is the culturally correct behaviour towards a leader.
- Shared technical education is at the same time a **strength**: the team has a common professional language (Scrum artefacts, code, architecture) which partly compensates for the cultural distance.

**Personality Diversity (DISC):**

Belbin describes *behaviour in a team*, not personality. To cover the personality dimension, the DISC model is used (D = Dominance, I = Influence, S = Steadiness, C = Conscientiousness).

| Role | DISC type | Short description |
| ---- | --------- | ----------------- |
| PO | **C** (with D) | Structured, quality-driven, wants rules and clear criteria; decides quickly once the facts are clear |
| SM | **I** (with S) | Open, communicative, motivating, informal; works through relationships |
| Dev1 | **C** | Analytical, precise, cautious; wants to be technically correct before speaking |
| Dev2 | **D** (with S) | Result-oriented, pushes topics forward, but adapts to the group |
| Dev3 | **C** (with S) | Thorough, reliable, calm; avoids risk and quick changes |
| Dev4 | **S** | Patient, loyal, harmony-seeking; rarely takes a position on his own |
| Dev5 | **S** (with C) | Steady, dependable, prefers clear processes and predictable work |
| Dev6 | **I** (with D) | Enthusiastic, spontaneous, likes new tools; low patience for detail |

- The team is clearly **dominated by S and C types** (Dev1, Dev3, Dev4, Dev5 and the PO). Only the SM and Dev6 are I types, only Dev2 and Dev6 carry a D share.
- Effect on interaction: S types avoid open confrontation, C types only speak when they are certain. Both hold back in discussions → **discussions produce little contradiction**.
- This is the same effect that already follows from the missing *Monitor Evaluator* (Belbin) and from high power distance / avoidant disagreement (culture). **Personality, team roles and culture reinforce each other in the same direction**, which makes the risk of *pseudo-harmony* (apparent consensus without real agreement) much higher than each factor alone would suggest.
- The SM is the only strong I type and is the person responsible for energising the team — but I types draw their strength from live interaction, which the async-only setup largely removes. Her natural style is therefore **weakened by the collaboration model**.
- Dev6 (I/D) and Dev1/Dev3 (C) are a predictable friction pair: fast, enthusiastic proposals meet a demand for evidence and detail. This is *task* friction, not personal, and can be productive if it is made visible.
- Note: DISC describes preferred behaviour, not fixed character. It is used here as an additional lens, not as a classification of the individuals.

Project: The team develops a mobile fitness app over 8 months, which is a tight deadline.

**Team Purpose, Deliverables and Performance Expectations:**

- Primary objective: deliver a market-ready mobile fitness app for a **fixed launch date** 8 months after project start (start June 2026, launch February 2027).
- Process framework: Scrum with **3-week sprints → approximately 11 sprints** in total.
- The deadline is fixed, the **scope is flexible**. Features are prioritised (MoSCoW) and cut if necessary; the date is not negotiable.

Product scope (three feature blocks):

| Block | Content | Main technical risk |
| ----- | ------- | ------------------- |
| 1. Workout tracking + progress statistics | Core function: recording workouts, history, progress charts | Base for everything else, must be stable early |
| 2. Wearable / sensor integration | Connection to smartwatches and fitness trackers, health data | Hardware fragmentation, expert knowledge required (Dev1) |
| 3. Social / community features | Profiles, feed, challenges, following other users | Personal data, GDPR and four different data protection regimes (Dev3) |

Milestones:

| Milestone | Sprint | Deliverable |
| --------- | ------ | ----------- |
| M1 | End of Sprint 2 | Architecture decided, tech stack fixed, team charter agreed |
| M2 | End of Sprint 5 | Internal alpha: tracking + statistics working |
| M3 | End of Sprint 8 | Beta: wearable integration, external test users |
| M4 | End of Sprint 10 | Feature freeze, social features complete |
| M5 | Sprint 11 | Release candidate, app store submission, launch |

Performance expectations:
- Potentially shippable increment at the end of every sprint
- Stable velocity from Sprint 3 onwards (the first two sprints are expected to be unreliable due to the forming stage)
- Definition of Done: code reviewed by a second developer, automated tests written, documentation in English, no known critical bugs
- Crash-free rate above 99 % at launch

Consequences for the team dynamics:
- **Only ~11 sprints exist.** The extended forming stage (see below) consumes an estimated 2–3 sprints, i.e. roughly **25 % of the entire project** is spent before the team works productively. This makes trust-building not a "soft" topic but a schedule-critical one.
- **Fixed date + flexible scope forces continuous re-prioritisation.** This is exactly the point where the short-term oriented members (SM, Dev6 → ship fast, accept technical debt) and the long-term oriented members (PO, Dev2, Dev4, Dev5 → proper architecture first) collide. The conflict is therefore not accidental but **built into the project setup** and will repeat in every sprint planning.
- **Flexible scope conflicts with high uncertainty avoidance** (Japan 92, Germany 65). Dev2, Dev4, Dev5 and the PO need detailed acceptance criteria, while the scope keeps changing. The strict Definition of Done above is a deliberate countermeasure.
- **Full-stack staffing does not remove all dependencies.** Blocks 2 and 3 require the expert knowledge of Dev1 and Dev3 — both in India, both in the time zone furthest from the US members. The two most critical feature blocks depend on the two members who, culturally, are least likely to raise a problem early.
- Eleven sprint reviews and retrospectives are the main synchronisation points of the team — but no sprint event can be attended live by all eight members (see time zones). Every single one of these 11 opportunities is therefore already compromised.

Critical dynamics we are going to investigate:

\- Indians tend not to respect women in leading positions

\- Germans tend to give more direct feedback, while Japanese tend to avoid it, as it is perceived as being rude

\- trust-building is difficult for a new team that never meets in person

\- because of the different time zones, it will never be possible for all team members to join a meeting. We will investigate, what methods the team will use to bridge that gap. Perhaps they could rely less on real-time communication, e. g. MS Teams meetings, and instead focus on methods which don't require all team members to be online at the same time.

**How the team was formed:**

- All eight members are **employees of the same company**, working from four different sites (Germany, USA, India, Japan). There are no freelancers and no outsourcing partners. This is a stabilising factor: shared HR framework, shared tooling, shared corporate language (English) and shared company processes.
- The selection criterion was **availability, not fit**: the project was staffed with whoever was free from other projects at that moment. Neither the Belbin balance nor the intercultural composition was planned.
  - This directly explains the role gaps analysed below: the missing *Plant* and *Monitor Evaluator* and the fourfold *Implementer* are not a design decision but an **accident of resource planning**.
  - The intercultural diversity of the team is therefore a **by-product, not a strategy**. The company did not compose a global team in order to build a global product — the global composition simply happened. Any innovation benefit from diversity is available, but nobody planned for it and nobody is managing it.
- The **PO and the SM were assigned top-down by management**, while the **six developers volunteered** for the project.
  - Strength: the developers have intrinsic motivation for the product. They chose the task.
  - Weakness: they chose the *project*, not the *people*. They did not choose their leaders and they did not choose each other.
  - The two female leaders derive their authority purely from a formal management decision. For the members from high power distance cultures (Dev1, Dev3, PDI 77) formal position is in principle a valid source of authority — but the management that appointed them is invisible in daily work. The result is **formal authority without earned personal authority**, which is precisely the gap in which the gender and age authority tension described later becomes effective.
- The team has **no shared history**: no previous project together, no established norms, no team memory. Nothing can be assumed as known; every working agreement has to be negotiated explicitly.
- Because the developers were released from other projects rather than moved permanently, they keep a **line manager and a home team at their own site**. This creates a matrix situation with dual loyalty and competing priorities, which the PO cannot control from Germany.
- Theoretical classification: this is a classic **"swift trust" situation** (Meyerson et al.). A temporary, distributed team without a common past cannot build trust through personal experience in the available time. It must initially rely on **role-based trust** — trusting the role and the professional competence rather than the person. This is why clear roles, reliability and visible delivery matter more here than social bonding activities.

**Leadership Styles:**

*Leadership constellation:*

- The team has **only formal leadership**. No informal leaders have emerged — the team is too young, has no shared history and communicates almost exclusively in writing, which gives no one the visibility needed to build informal influence.
- Leadership is **not centralized in one person but split between two formal roles**, as Scrum prescribes: the PO owns the content ("what"), the SM owns the process ("how").
- In practice this split does not work. The findings already collected in this analysis show that leadership is **formally divided but functionally unclear**:
  - "Some roles overlap which leads to confusion"
  - "Decision making isn't clear" / "No structured decision-making process established"
  - "PO could disagree with the leadership style of SM … which leads to conflicts when leading the team"
- The result is **dual leadership without a defined boundary** — two leaders, two styles, no rule for who decides what.

*Scrum Master — Servant Leadership (Greenleaf):*

- The SM leads by serving: she removes impediments, facilitates, asks what the team needs, and does not give directions.
- This is consistent with her Belbin roles (Teamworker, Coordinator, Resource Investigator), her DISC profile (I — relationship-driven, informal) and her cultural background (USA: PDI 40, Indulgence 68).
- **Effectiveness — positive:** works well for Dev5 (Germany, PDI 35) and Dev6 (USA, PDI 40), who expect autonomy and are used to being asked for their opinion.
- **Effectiveness — negative:** for Dev1 and Dev3 (India, PDI 77) and Dev2 and Dev4 (Japan, PDI 54, UAI 92) the same behaviour is read as an **absence of leadership**. A leader who asks instead of deciding appears uncertain or weak. The question "What do you need?" produces silence, not answers — the very effect described under Power Distance above.
- **Structural weakness:** servant leadership depends on frequent, informal, personal interaction. The async-only setup across four time zones removes exactly that channel. The SM's natural style is therefore weakened by the collaboration model (see also her DISC I profile).

*Product Owner — Directive Leadership:*

- The PO sets priorities, defines acceptance criteria, decides on content and expects the decisions to be executed.
- This is consistent with her Belbin roles (Coordinator, Completer Finisher), her DISC profile (C with D — structure and quality first) and her cultural background (Germany: UAI 65, LTO 83, low Indulgence 40 → duty, discipline, formality).
- **Effectiveness — positive:** clear instructions reduce uncertainty. This fits the expectations of the high power distance members and especially serves the very high uncertainty avoidance of the Japanese members (UAI 92).
- **Effectiveness — negative:** it collides with the high individualism of the US members (IDV 91). The SM and Dev6 expect participation and autonomy and experience directive prioritisation as being overruled.
- **Effectiveness — critical:** a directive style makes the authority claim maximally visible. A 30-year-old female leader instructing a 65-year-old male specialist from a high power distance culture puts age, gender and hierarchy in direct opposition in every single interaction. The directive style therefore **amplifies the gender and age authority conflict instead of defusing it**.

*Evaluation of the combination:*

| | Scrum Master | Product Owner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Style | Servant | Directive |
| Signal to the team | "Decide together, I support you" | "I decide, please implement" |
| Works for | Germany, USA (low PDI, high IDV) | India, Japan (high PDI, high UAI) |
| Fails for | India, Japan | USA (high IDV) |

- The two styles are **opposite ends of the same scale**. The team receives contradictory signals: the SM invites open discussion, the PO expects execution.
- For members from high power distance cultures, contradictory signals from two leaders are especially damaging. They need to know *who* the authority is. When two leaders visibly disagree, the culturally safe behaviour is to wait and say nothing. This is a direct explanation for the already documented finding that **decision-making is unclear and decisions stall**.
- Neither style supplies a decision-making *process*: directive leadership means one person decides, servant leadership means the group decides. Because neither is written down, nobody knows which rule applies to which type of decision.

*Assessment using Situational Leadership (Hersey & Blanchard):*

- The individual technical maturity of the team is high, but the **maturity as a team is low** (forming stage, no shared norms, no trust). The model therefore recommends a **high task orientation** at this point: clear structures, explicit rules, close guidance — and a shift towards supporting and delegating only as team maturity grows.
- Conclusion: the PO's directive style is **appropriate for the current stage**, and the SM's servant style is **appropriate for a later stage**. The problem is not the styles themselves but that both are applied **simultaneously to the same team** instead of sequentially.
- Derived recommendation: agree explicitly that leadership is directive and structure-giving until roughly Sprint 4 (until the norming stage is reached), and shift deliberately towards servant leadership afterwards. This has to be communicated openly so that it is not perceived as an arbitrary change of style.
- Second derived recommendation: define **explicit decision rules** (who decides what alone, what is decided jointly, what is escalated) and document them in the team charter. Without this, neither leadership style can be effective.
