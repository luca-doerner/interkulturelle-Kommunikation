- Consequence 3 (risk): there is **no dedicated QA role**. Quality relies entirely on the developers themselves — which combines badly with the missing *Monitor Evaluator* (no critical review) and the overrepresentation of *Completer Finisher* (detail work without an independent quality gate).
- Consequence 4 (conflict potential): the two informal experts (Dev1, 55 and Dev3, 65, both India, PDI 77) are the oldest members of the team and report to a 30-year-old female PO and a 45-year-old female SM from low power distance cultures. **Expertise, age and gender authority therefore point in opposite directions** — this intensifies the authority tension described in the conflict section.

**Personality Diversity (DISC):**

Belbin describes *behaviour in a team*, not personality. To cover the personality dimension, the DISC model is used (D = Dominance, I = Influence, S = Steadiness, C = Conscientiousness).

| Role | DISC type | Short description |
| ---- | --------- | ----------------- |
| PO | **C** (with D) | Structured, quality-driven, wants rules and clear criteria; decides quickly once the facts are clear |
| SM | **I** (with S) | Open, communicative, motivating, informal; works through relationships |
| Dev1 | **C** | Analytical, precise, cautious; wants to be technically correct before speaking |
| Dev2 | **D** (with S) | Result-oriented, pushes topics forward, but adapts to the group |
| Dev3 | **C** (with S) | Thorough, reliable, calm; avoids risk and quick changes |
| Dev4 | **S** | Patient, loyal, harmony-seeking; rarely takes a position on his own |
| Dev5 | **S** (with C) | Steady, dependable, prefers clear processes and predictable work |
| Dev6 | **I** (with D) | Enthusiastic, spontaneous, likes new tools; low patience for detail |

- The team is clearly **dominated by S and C types** (Dev1, Dev3, Dev4, Dev5 and the PO). Only the SM and Dev6 are I types, only Dev2 and Dev6 carry a D share.
- Effect on interaction: S types avoid open confrontation, C types only speak when they are certain. Both hold back in discussions → **discussions produce little contradiction**.
- This is the same effect that already follows from the missing *Monitor Evaluator* (Belbin) and from high power distance / avoidant disagreement (culture). **Personality, team roles and culture reinforce each other in the same direction**, which makes the risk of *pseudo-harmony* (apparent consensus without real agreement) much higher than each factor alone would suggest.
- The SM is the only strong I type and is the person responsible for energising the team — but I types draw their strength from live interaction, which the async-only setup largely removes. Her natural style is therefore **weakened by the collaboration model**.
- Dev6 (I/D) and Dev1/Dev3 (C) are a predictable friction pair: fast, enthusiastic proposals meet a demand for evidence and detail. This is *task* friction, not personal, and can be productive if it is made visible.
- Note: DISC describes preferred behaviour, not fixed character. It is used here as an additional lens, not as a classification of the individuals.

**Leadership Styles:**

*Leadership constellation:*

- The team has **only formal leadership**. No informal leaders have emerged — the team is too young, has no shared history and communicates almost exclusively in writing, which gives no one the visibility needed to build informal influence.
- Leadership is **not centralized in one person but split between two formal roles**, as Scrum prescribes: the PO owns the content ("what"), the SM owns the process ("how").
- In practice this split does not work. The findings already collected in this analysis show that leadership is **formally divided but functionally unclear**:
  - "Some roles overlap which leads to confusion"
  - "Decision making isn't clear" / "No structured decision-making process established"
  - "PO could disagree with the leadership style of SM … which leads to conflicts when leading the team"
- The result is **dual leadership without a defined boundary** — two leaders, two styles, no rule for who decides what.

*Scrum Master — Servant Leadership (Greenleaf):*

- The SM leads by serving: she removes impediments, facilitates, asks what the team needs, and does not give directions.
- This is consistent with her Belbin roles (Teamworker, Coordinator, Resource Investigator), her DISC profile (I — relationship-driven, informal) and her cultural background (USA: PDI 40, Indulgence 68).
- **Effectiveness — positive:** works well for Dev5 (Germany, PDI 35) and Dev6 (USA, PDI 40), who expect autonomy and are used to being asked for their opinion.
- **Effectiveness — negative:** for Dev1 and Dev3 (India, PDI 77) and Dev2 and Dev4 (Japan, PDI 54, UAI 92) the same behaviour is read as an **absence of leadership**. A leader who asks instead of deciding appears uncertain or weak. The question "What do you need?" produces silence, not answers — the very effect described under Power Distance above.
- **Structural weakness:** servant leadership depends on frequent, informal, personal interaction. The async-only setup across four time zones removes exactly that channel. The SM's natural style is therefore weakened by the collaboration model (see also her DISC I profile).

*Product Owner — Directive Leadership:*

- The PO sets priorities, defines acceptance criteria, decides on content and expects the decisions to be executed.
- This is consistent with her Belbin roles (Coordinator, Completer Finisher), her DISC profile (C with D — structure and quality first) and her cultural background (Germany: UAI 65, LTO 83, low Indulgence 40 → duty, discipline, formality).
- **Effectiveness — positive:** clear instructions reduce uncertainty. This fits the expectations of the high power distance members and especially serves the very high uncertainty avoidance of the Japanese members (UAI 92).
- **Effectiveness — negative:** it collides with the high individualism of the US members (IDV 91). The SM and Dev6 expect participation and autonomy and experience directive prioritisation as being overruled.
- **Effectiveness — critical:** a directive style makes the authority claim maximally visible. A 30-year-old female leader instructing a 65-year-old male specialist from a high power distance culture puts age, gender and hierarchy in direct opposition in every single interaction. The directive style therefore **amplifies the gender and age authority conflict instead of defusing it**.

*Evaluation of the combination:*

| | Scrum Master | Product Owner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Style | Servant | Directive |
| Signal to the team | "Decide together, I support you" | "I decide, please implement" |
| Works for | Germany, USA (low PDI, high IDV) | India, Japan (high PDI, high UAI) |
| Fails for | India, Japan | USA (high IDV) |

- The two styles are **opposite ends of the same scale**. The team receives contradictory signals: the SM invites open discussion, the PO expects execution.
- For members from high power distance cultures, contradictory signals from two leaders are especially damaging. They need to know *who* the authority is. When two leaders visibly disagree, the culturally safe behaviour is to wait and say nothing. This is a direct explanation for the already documented finding that **decision-making is unclear and decisions stall**.
- Neither style supplies a decision-making *process*: directive leadership means one person decides, servant leadership means the group decides. Because neither is written down, nobody knows which rule applies to which type of decision.

*Assessment using Situational Leadership (Hersey & Blanchard):*

- The individual technical maturity of the team is high, but the **maturity as a team is low** (forming stage, no shared norms, no trust). The model therefore recommends a **high task orientation** at this point: clear structures, explicit rules, close guidance — and a shift towards supporting and delegating only as team maturity grows.
- Conclusion: the PO's directive style is **appropriate for the current stage**, and the SM's servant style is **appropriate for a later stage**. The problem is not the styles themselves but that both are applied **simultaneously to the same team** instead of sequentially.
- Derived recommendation: agree explicitly that leadership is directive and structure-giving until roughly Sprint 4 (until the norming stage is reached), and shift deliberately towards servant leadership afterwards. This has to be communicated openly so that it is not perceived as an arbitrary change of style.
- Second derived recommendation: define **explicit decision rules** (who decides what alone, what is decided jointly, what is escalated) and document them in the team charter. Without this, neither leadership style can be effective.

- Action Plan:
  - Action: Establish async communication protocol + virtual team charter with explicit intercultural norms
  - Responsible: Scrum Master
  - Expected Impact: Improved trust, clearer expectations, reduced misunderstandings across cultures and time zones
- One of the most important improvements for the team:
  - Prioritize trust-building and cultural awareness through a team charter and async-first communication strategy
  - This is the foundation for all other improvements in this globally distributed Scrum team

**Conflict:**

*1. Classification of conflict types:*

The conflicts collected below can be sorted into four types. The distinction matters because task and process conflicts can be productive, while relationship conflicts are almost always destructive.

| Type | Conflicts in this team | Assessment |
| ---- | ---------------------- | ---------- |
| **Task conflict** (content, goals, priorities) | Short-term vs. long-term orientation when prioritising the backlog (SM/Dev6 want a fast MVP, PO/Dev2/Dev4/Dev5 want proper architecture); level of detail of acceptance criteria and Definition of Done (high vs. low uncertainty avoidance); technical proposals from Dev6 (I/D) vs. demand for evidence from Dev1/Dev3 (C) | **Potentially productive** — this is where diversity creates better solutions, provided the conflicts become visible at all |
| **Process conflict** (how work is organised) | Unclear decision-making, no structured decision process, overlapping roles, unstructured meetings, different expectations regarding punctuality, informality and meeting structure, no accountability for components (full-stack, no ownership) | **Currently the largest block** and mostly solvable through explicit agreements |
| **Relationship conflict** (persons, trust, respect) | Low initial trust (never met in person), authority tension around the female PO and SM, age vs. hierarchy (Dev3 is 65 and reports to a 30-year-old PO), perception of the SM's informal style as disrespectful | **Most dangerous type** — cannot be solved by rules, requires trust-building |
| **Cultural conflict** (norms, values, communication) | Direct German feedback vs. Japanese avoidance of negative feedback, silence read as agreement, public exposure of individuals as loss of face for the Indian and Japanese members, contradictory expectations towards leadership behaviour | **Underlies all other types** — cultural differences frequently cause task and process conflicts to be *misinterpreted as* relationship conflicts |

- The central risk of this team is **escalation across types**: a task conflict (which architecture?) that is expressed in a very direct German manner is perceived by a Japanese team member as a personal attack and therefore becomes a relationship conflict. What began as a productive difference of opinion ends as damaged trust.
- Because the team is in the forming stage with low trust, this transition happens especially easily — there is no relationship buffer that would allow a harsh remark to be absorbed as "he means it professionally, not personally".

*2. Conflict handling styles (Thomas-Kilmann):*

The team splits into two camps along cultural lines. The Thomas-Kilmann model distinguishes five styles along the axes assertiveness and cooperativeness: competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, accommodating.

| Member | Dominant style | Cultural / personality basis |
| ------ | -------------- | ---------------------------- |
| PO (DE) | **Competing** | Confrontational disagreement, very direct feedback, DISC C/D, directive leadership |
| Dev5 (DE) | Competing / Collaborating | Very low context, confrontational, but DISC S softens it |
| SM (US) | **Collaborating** (intended) | Servant leadership, DISC I, seeks joint solutions |
| Dev6 (US) | Competing / Collaborating | Moderately avoidant culturally, but DISC I/D and high individualism |
| Dev1 (IN) | **Avoiding** | Avoidant disagreement, PDI 77, DISC C |
| Dev3 (IN) | **Avoiding / Accommodating** | Avoidant, PDI 77, DISC C/S, high seniority but culturally reserved towards leaders |
| Dev2 (JP) | **Avoiding** | Strongly avoidant, very high context, DISC D but adapts to the group |
| Dev4 (JP) | **Accommodating** | Strongly avoidant, UAI 92, DISC S — gives in to preserve harmony |

Consequences of this split:

- The **German and US members address conflicts openly**, the **Indian and Japanese members avoid or accommodate**. A conflict therefore never has two visible parties: one side argues, the other side goes quiet.
- The confronting side interprets silence as **agreement** and considers the conflict resolved. The avoiding side interprets the confrontation as **aggression** and considers the conflict unresolved but not discussable. Both sides leave the meeting with opposite conclusions.
- This produces exactly the **pseudo-harmony** already described: an impression of consensus without an actual consensus. Decisions appear to be agreed, but are not carried by half the team — and re-emerge later as delays, rework or silent non-implementation.
- Accommodating (Dev4) is particularly problematic in Scrum: he will agree to sprint commitments he considers unrealistic rather than object during planning. The problem only becomes visible at the sprint review, when the increment is incomplete.
- The SM's collaborating style is the theoretically correct approach for this team, but collaborating requires **all parties to state their position**. Against a culturally avoiding majority the style cannot work — the SM ends up mediating between one loud side and one silent side.
- The async, written communication reinforces the split further: avoiding a conflict is far easier in writing (simply not replying, replying non-committally, or replying much later) than in a live conversation.

*3. How conflicts are currently resolved — and the accountability gap:*

- **There is no conflict resolution process at all.** No agreed escalation path, no defined mediator role, no retrospective format designed to surface conflicts safely, no rule for what happens when the PO and the SM disagree.
- Conflicts are therefore **not resolved but absorbed**: they disappear from the visible communication and remain effective as reduced commitment, silent non-implementation and declining trust.
- Authority, decision-making and accountability are equally undefined:
  - **Authority** is formally assigned (PO decides content, SM owns process) but not accepted in practice — it rests on a management decision and is undermined by gender, age and the contradictory leadership styles.
  - **Decision-making** has no rule: it is not defined which decisions the PO makes alone, which the team makes jointly, and which are escalated. Directive and servant leadership imply *different* answers to this question, and neither is written down.
  - **Accountability** is diffuse: full-stack staffing without component ownership means no individual is responsible for any part of the product. In combination with the missing QA role and the missing Monitor Evaluator, **quality problems have no owner**.
- Assessment: the absence of these three definitions is not a separate problem but the **common root cause** of most conflicts listed below. As long as it is unclear who decides and who is accountable, every task conflict remains open and every process conflict recurs.

**Collaboration Tools and model:**

*1. Time zone analysis — proof that no common meeting slot exists:*

Assumed locations (June 2026, summer time): Germany CEST = UTC+2, USA East Coast EDT = UTC−4, India IST = UTC+5:30, Japan JST = UTC+9. This spans **13 hours** between the westernmost and easternmost member.

Regular working hours 09:00–17:00 local, converted to UTC:

| Site | Members | Local working hours | In UTC |
| ---- | ------- | ------------------- | ------ |
| USA (UTC−4) | SM, Dev6 | 09:00–17:00 | 13:00–21:00 |
| Germany (UTC+2) | PO, Dev5 | 09:00–17:00 | 07:00–15:00 |
| India (UTC+5:30) | Dev1, Dev3 | 09:00–17:00 | 03:30–11:30 |
| Japan (UTC+9) | Dev2, Dev4 | 09:00–17:00 | 00:00–08:00 |

- Germany ∩ USA = 13:00–15:00 UTC
- India ends at 11:30 UTC, Japan ends at 08:00 UTC
- **Intersection of all four = empty.** Even with extended availability (08:00–19:00 local) the intersection remains empty.
- The decisive gap is **USA ↔ Japan**: the Japanese working day ends at 08:00 UTC, the US working day starts at 13:00 UTC — a permanent gap of five hours. No adjustment of meeting culture can close it.

Which subsets *can* meet:

| Group | Common window (UTC) | Local times |
| ----- | ------------------- | ----------- |
| Germany + India + Japan | 07:00–08:00 | DE 09:00, IN 12:30, JP 16:00 |
| Germany + USA | 13:00–15:00 | DE 15:00, US 09:00 |
| Germany + India + USA | 13:00–13:30 (extended) | DE 15:00, IN 18:30, US 09:00 |
| **All four** | **none** | — |

- The "least bad" all-hands slot is **13:00 UTC** = Germany 15:00, USA 09:00, India 18:30, **Japan 22:00**. Any synchronous full-team meeting requires the Japanese members to join late in the evening.
- This is not a neutral inconvenience: Japan has the highest masculinity score (95) and the highest uncertainty avoidance (92) in the team. Dev2 and Dev4 will **accept** the evening slot rather than object (accommodating style), so the cost stays invisible while resentment builds.
- Conclusion: synchronous full-team collaboration is **structurally impossible**, not merely difficult. The team cannot fix this with better scheduling — it must change its collaboration model.

*2. Tool landscape:*

| Tool | Purpose | Mode |
| ---- | ------- | ---- |
| **MS Teams** | Chat, channels, video calls, recordings | Sync + async |
| **Jira** | Backlog, sprint board, task ownership, status | Async |
| **Confluence** | Team charter, decisions, architecture documentation, meeting notes | Async |
| **Git + CI pipeline** | Code, pull requests, code review, automated tests and builds | Async |

How the tools support the team:

- **Knowledge sharing:** Confluence replaces the corridor conversation that does not exist in this team. Because there is no shared history and no informal channel, anything not written down does not exist for at least half the team.
- **Coordination:** Jira makes the invisible visible. It is the only place where the diffuse accountability identified above (full-stack, no component ownership) can be repaired — through explicit assignees per ticket.
- **Cohesion:** this is where the tools are weakest. Jira and Git support the task level, not the relationship level. **No tool in the landscape builds trust**, which is the team's central problem.
- **Quality:** the CI pipeline and mandatory pull requests are a partial compensation for the missing QA role and the missing Belbin *Monitor Evaluator* — an automated, culturally neutral reviewer that rejects code without anyone losing face. This is a genuine advantage in a high-context, face-sensitive team.

*3. Collaboration model: async-first*

Since no synchronous slot exists, the team works **async-first**: synchronous communication is the exception and must be justified, not the default.

| Practice | Implementation | Bridges |
| -------- | -------------- | ------- |
| **Written daily standup** | Every member posts status, plan and impediments in a Teams channel by the end of their own working day | Time zones — no one has to be awake outside working hours |
| **Recorded meetings** | Sprint review and planning are recorded, with written summaries and decisions in Confluence within 24 hours | Time zones + language: non-native speakers can re-watch and re-read |
| **Written decisions** | Every decision is documented with reasoning and a named decision-maker | The unclear decision-making identified in the conflict analysis |
| **Written feedback rounds** | Comments on tickets and pull requests instead of live criticism | The direct/indirect feedback conflict — written feedback is less confrontational and preserves face |
| **Follow-the-sun handover** | End-of-day handover note per ticket; full-stack staffing makes this possible | Turns the 13-hour spread from a defect into ~20 hours of daily coverage |

Critical evaluation of the async-first model:

- **Strengths:** it removes the structural time zone problem instead of managing it. It suits the high-uncertainty-avoidance members (everything is documented and explicit) and gives non-native speakers time to formulate carefully. Written asynchronous discussion also weakens the dominance of the most confrontational speakers — the quiet members get a channel where they are not talked over.
- **Weakness 1 — trust:** async communication is efficient for tasks but poor for relationships. The team's biggest problem is trust, and async-first does the least for exactly that. It must therefore be paired with deliberate synchronous relationship time (e.g. rotating pairwise calls), otherwise it optimises the wrong dimension.
- **Weakness 2 — it does not solve avoidance, it makes it easier.** As noted in the conflict analysis, staying silent is simpler in writing than in a live meeting. Async-first must be combined with a rule that forces an explicit statement (e.g. every team member must respond to a decision proposal within 24 hours, and silence is *not* counted as agreement).
- **Weakness 3 — the SM's leadership style is weakened.** Servant leadership and her DISC I profile both depend on personal interaction, which async-first reduces further.
- **Weakness 4 — speed:** a question that would take two minutes live can take a full day asynchronously. With only 11 sprints and a fixed launch date, this latency is a real schedule risk and requires clear rules on when synchronous escalation is allowed.

*2. Major challenges and risks:*

| # | Risk | Cause | Likelihood | Impact |
| - | ---- | ----- | ---------- | ------ |
| R1 | **Pseudo-harmony**: decisions appear agreed but are not carried | High PDI + avoidant disagreement + S/C personalities + missing Monitor Evaluator, all pointing the same way | **High** | High — rework, silent non-implementation, deadline risk |

| R4 | **Authority erosion of PO and SM** | Formal appointment without earned authority, gender and age tension, contradictory leadership signals | Medium–high | High — without accepted leadership no measure takes effect |

*3. Diversity as a driver of innovation and performance:*

Diversity in this team is currently almost exclusively a **cost**. Its potential value is real but unrealised, because the team was staffed by availability and nobody is managing the diversity.

Where diversity creates measurable value:

- **Product quality for a global market:** the app is to be launched internationally. Fitness culture, privacy expectations and social feature acceptance differ strongly between Germany, the USA, India and Japan. The team can validate assumptions internally that a monocultural team would only discover after launch. The social features (block 3) in particular touch data protection expectations that differ fundamentally between the four regions.
- **Four-way coverage of working time:** the 13-hour spread is a defect for synchronous work and an asset for asynchronous work. With full-stack staffing and handover notes, the team achieves roughly 20 hours of daily coverage — critical bugs before launch can be worked on almost around the clock.
- **Better decisions through cognitive diversity:** different educational systems and cultural problem-solving approaches (principles-first vs. applications-first, long-term vs. short-term) produce a broader solution space. The recurring backlog conflict between "ship fast" and "build properly" is not a defect — it is the mechanism that prevents both extremes.
- **Complementary Belbin and DISC profiles:** Dev6 (I/D, resource investigator) generates options, Dev1 and Dev3 (C, specialists) validate them. This pairing produces better results than either profile alone.

The decisive condition:

- Diversity only improves performance when **differences become visible and are discussed**. In a team where half the members avoid disagreement, diversity is present but **inaccessible** — the different perspectives exist and never enter the decision.
- The team therefore currently pays the full cost of diversity (coordination effort, misunderstandings, longer forming stage) while receiving almost none of the benefit.
- **All recommendations below aim at exactly this conversion**: making existing diversity usable rather than adding more of it.

*4. Recommendations:*

| # | Area | Measure | Owner | Expected impact | Timing |
| - | ---- | ------- | ----- | --------------- | ------ |
| E1 | Collaboration | **Virtual team charter**: written working agreements on availability, response times, meeting norms, punctuality, language, escalation | SM | Replaces implicit cultural expectations with explicit shared ones; addresses R3, R6 | Sprint 1, review each quarter |
| E2 | Leadership | **Explicit decision rules**: define which decisions the PO makes alone, which are made jointly, which are escalated — documented in Confluence with a named decision-maker per decision | PO + SM | Removes the core cause of R3 and R4; gives high-PDI members the unambiguous authority they need | Sprint 1 |
| E3 | Leadership | **Sequential leadership style**: agree openly on a directive, structure-giving style until roughly Sprint 4, then a deliberate shift towards servant leadership as team maturity grows (Hersey & Blanchard) | PO + SM | Ends the contradictory signals; matches leadership to the actual team maturity; addresses R4 | Sprint 1, shift at Sprint 4 |
| E4 | Communication | **Silence is not agreement**: every decision proposal is posted in writing; every member must respond explicitly within 24 hours ("agree" / "concern" / "question") | SM | Directly attacks R1; forces the avoiding members into an explicit position without public confrontation | Sprint 1 |
| E5 | Communication | **Feedback protocol**: negative feedback in writing on tickets and pull requests, never publicly against a person; criticism refers to artefacts, not people; no public exposure in reviews | SM | Defuses R2 at its source; protects face for the Indian and Japanese members while preserving German directness on content | Sprint 1 |
| E6 | Collaboration | **Async-first as the default**, plus a fair meeting rotation: full-team meetings rotate so the burden of unfavourable times is shared instead of always falling on Japan | SM | Solves the structural time zone problem; addresses R7 and makes the hidden cost visible | Sprint 1 |
| E7 | Effectiveness | **Trust-building programme**: rotating pairwise 30-minute calls between all members over the first sprints, plus one synchronous kick-off with cameras on; purely relational, no agenda | SM | The only measure aimed at R2 and the trust deficit; compensates for the weakness of async-first | Sprints 1–3 |
| E8 | Effectiveness | **Compensate for the missing Belbin roles**: assign the *Monitor Evaluator* function as a rotating role in every sprint review (a nominated devil's advocate), and reserve time for open solution search before committing (*Plant* substitute) | SM | Addresses R8 and R5; a rotating, assigned role legitimises criticism culturally — the person is fulfilling a duty, not attacking anyone | From Sprint 2 |

*5. Prioritisation and conclusion:*

- Highest priority: **E2 (decision rules), E4 (silence is not agreement) and E7 (trust-building)**. E2 and E4 attack the root causes of the largest risk cluster (R1, R3), while E7 is the only measure addressing the trust deficit, which no rule can fix.
- E8 deserves particular attention because it uses a **cultural mechanism instead of fighting one**: in high power distance and face-sensitive cultures, criticism is far easier to express when it is a formally assigned role rather than a personal initiative.
- Overall assessment: the team's problems are **serious but solvable**. They are almost entirely problems of missing explicit agreements — decision rules, feedback norms, availability, accountability — and not problems of competence, motivation or goodwill. The professional foundation (expertise, motivation, tooling, one company) is strong.
- The critical constraint is **time**: with 11 sprints and a fixed launch date, the measures must take effect within the first two sprints. Trust-building and cultural agreements are therefore not soft, optional topics but the schedule-critical path of this project.
